## A. Executive Research Summary

Financial markets are organised places or systems where people, businesses, governments and institutions trade financial claims—such as deposits, shares, bonds, currencies and derivatives. Their core economic roles are to connect savers with borrowers, help set prices through buying and selling, provide liquidity, transfer and price risk, and support investment and economic development.[^1_1][^1_2]

For Papua New Guinea, the financial system is anchored by the **Bank of Papua New Guinea (BPNG)**, seven licensed commercial banks, licensed financial institutions, savings and loan societies, payment providers, insurers and superannuation funds. PNG’s capital market is small but functioning: **PNGX Markets** is the country’s only stock exchange, regulated by the Securities Commission of PNG, with 13 listed companies as shown on PNGX’s current listing page.[^1_3][^1_4][^1_5]

Three PNG themes should shape the eBook:

1. **Commodity dependence:** Energy and mineral exports accounted for over 77% of PNG’s exports in 2024, making global prices for LNG, gold, copper, oil, coffee, cocoa and palm oil highly important to national income and foreign exchange.[^1_6]
2. **Foreign exchange reform:** PNG has faced persistent foreign-exchange shortages; the IMF reports improved access to FX following central-bank reforms and a gradual reduction of kina overvaluation, with kina convertibility targeted for return by end-2026 under the IMF-supported programme.[^1_7][^1_8]
3. **Financial inclusion and access:** PNG’s financial system includes commercial banks, microfinance-style licensed financial institutions, savings and loan societies, and mobile/payment providers, but rural access and financial literacy remain important constraints; PNG-specific current inclusion statistics require verification from BPNG and the National Statistical Office.[^1_3]

## B. Global Financial Markets

### What financial markets are

**GLOBAL FACT:** A financial market is any organised system where financial assets or claims are bought, sold, issued or priced. Examples include stock exchanges, bond markets, foreign-exchange markets, money markets and commodity markets. Authoritative financial education sources commonly define them as mechanisms that channel funds from those with surplus funds to those who need funds.[^1_1]

**Why they exist**

- They connect **savers**—households, firms and institutions with surplus money—to **borrowers**—governments, companies and households needing finance.
- They enable **capital allocation**: money can move toward projects and firms expected to generate returns.
- They support **price discovery**: buyers and sellers reveal willingness to pay or accept through transactions and orders.
- They provide **liquidity**: participants can usually convert assets into cash, though liquidity varies by asset and market conditions.
- They allow **risk transfer**: insurance, derivatives and diversified portfolios can shift, share or hedge risks.
- They enable **maturity transformation**: banks and markets convert short-term savings into longer-term loans or investments.

**Simple examples for later adaptation**

- A teacher saves in a bank account; the bank may lend those deposits to a local business.
- A company issues shares to raise money for expansion; investors become part-owners.
- The PNG Government issues Treasury bills to finance spending; investors receive a government debt instrument.[^1_9]
- A coffee exporter receiving US dollars may need to exchange them into kina, connecting the export economy to the foreign-exchange market.[^1_6]


### Core concepts

| Concept | Plain-language meaning | Source basis |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Price discovery | The process by which trading reveals a market price | Standard market-function concept; supported by exchange/regulator descriptions of orderly markets [^1_10][^1_5] |
| Liquidity | Ease of buying or selling without materially changing the price | Standard financial-market concept |
| Risk transfer | Moving or sharing financial risk through contracts such as insurance or derivatives | Standard financial-market concept |
| Maturity transformation | Using short-term funding to support longer-term lending or investment | Standard banking/finance concept |
| Capital allocation | Directing savings toward productive uses | Standard financial-development concept |

**Caution for the editor:** Do not claim that financial markets automatically produce growth. The relationship between financial development and growth is generally positive but nuanced; weak regulation, excessive credit growth, poor governance and crises can undermine benefits.

## C. Papua New Guinea Financial System

### Structure

**PNG-SPECIFIC FACT:** BPNG publishes an official list of authorised or licensed financial institutions. As at **29 July 2025**, the listed categories included commercial banks, licensed financial institutions, savings and loan societies, payment service providers, authorised money remitters, authorised foreign-exchange dealers, money changers, life-insurance businesses and licensed superannuation trustees.[^1_3]

### Commercial banks

As at 29 July 2025, BPNG listed seven commercial banks: ANZ PNG, BSP Financial Group, Credit Corporation Finance Limited trading as CreditBank PNG, Kina Securities, National Banking Corporation, TISA Bank and Westpac PNG.[^1_3]

Commercial banks accept deposits, make loans, operate payment services and provide foreign-exchange services. They are central to the eBook because they are the financial-market institution most PNG readers will encounter directly.

### Licensed financial institutions and microfinance

BPNG’s 2025 list includes 13 licensed financial institutions, including microfinance-oriented institutions such as Nationwide Microbank, Women’s Micro Bank, Kada Poroman Microfinance and Kwik Moni. These institutions can broaden access to deposits, credit and payments, particularly where conventional bank branches are limited; the eBook should avoid claiming that every institution provides identical services nationwide.[^1_3]

### Savings and loan societies

BPNG lists 18 savings and loan societies as at 29 July 2025. These societies are authorised to accept deposits from and lend to their members. They are a useful PNG-specific example of community- or workplace-based financial intermediation.[^1_3]

### Payments and mobile money

BPNG lists **Digicel (PNG) Financial Services Limited** as a payment service provider and as authorised for inward remittances. Mobile money is highly relevant to PNG because it can reduce the need for physical bank branches, but the dossier did not verify a current official national account-ownership or mobile-money usage statistic; this is a research gap.[^1_3]

### Superannuation and insurance

BPNG’s licensing categories include life-insurance businesses and licensed superannuation trustees. Superannuation is important because it pools long-term worker savings and can be a major domestic institutional investor, but the dossier did not verify current PNG superannuation assets under management; this requires BPNG or regulator verification.[^1_3]

### How the parts interact

A simplified PNG flow is:

1. Households and workers save through banks, savings and loan societies, mobile money and superannuation.
2. Banks and licensed financial institutions lend to households and businesses.
3. Businesses use finance for working capital, equipment, imports and expansion.
4. Government borrows through Treasury bills and inscribed stock/Treasury bonds, issued through BPNG as issuing agent.[^1_11][^1_9]
5. Exporters earn foreign currency, which affects kina liquidity and the foreign-exchange market.[^1_6]
6. BPNG regulates banks and payment providers, conducts monetary policy, manages currency issues and oversees financial stability.[^1_2][^1_1]

## D. PNG Capital and Securities Markets

### PNGX Markets

**PNG-SPECIFIC FACT / CURRENT FACT:** PNGX Markets Limited is PNG’s only stock exchange. It began operations in **1999** as the Port Moresby Stock Exchange and was renamed PNGX in **December 2018** after a change in ownership.[^1_5]

PNGX provides a formal marketplace where companies can raise money from investors and investors can buy and sell securities. It is regulated and licensed by the Securities Commission of Papua New Guinea.[^1_10][^1_5]

### Listed companies

PNGX’s official listed-companies page currently shows **13 companies**, including BSP Financial Group, Kina Securities, Credit Corporation, PNG Air, City Pharmacy, Steamships, Kina Asset Management, NGIP Argmark, Niuminco, Newmont Corporation, Santos, Pacific Lime and Cement, and Adyton Resources Corporation.[^1_4]

**Important dating note:** The PNGX page includes Adyton Resources Corporation with a listing date of **23 September 2026**, so the list should be re-checked immediately before publication.[^1_4]

### Securities traded

SCPNG states that securities traded in PNG include shares, corporate bonds and managed funds. PNGX also describes markets for equity capital and wholesale corporate bonds on its trading platform.[^1_12][^1_13]

### Regulation and market conduct

The Securities Commission of PNG is established under the **Securities Commission Act 2015** and enforces the **Capital Market Act 2015** and **Central Depositories Act 2015**. Its functions include approving market rules, licensing intermediaries, market surveillance, monitoring compliance and promoting investor protection.[^1_14][^1_5]

Regulated capital-market activities requiring SCPNG licences include stock and derivative exchanges, stock brokerage, clearing houses, advisory services, fund management, trustee services, managed investment schemes and stock registry services.[^1_15]

### Investor access

**PNG-SPECIFIC FACT:** Investors cannot buy shares directly from PNGX; they must use an authorised stockbroker. SCPNG identifies JMP Securities Limited and Kina Securities Limited as licensed brokers and dealers facilitating securities transactions.[^1_5][^1_12]

**Accessibility issues to research further:** minimum investment amounts, brokerage fees, digital access, rural access, financial literacy, and whether ordinary investors can readily access corporate bonds. These practical barriers are not fully documented in the sources retrieved and should not be assumed.

### Market size and activity

An SCPNG page states PNGX listed 13 companies with total market capitalisation of **US\$20.3 billion as of October 2021**. This is **outdated** and should not be presented as current. A current market-capitalisation figure was not verified in the retrieved sources; obtain it directly from PNGX market data before publication.[^1_16]

## E. Banking and Monetary System

### Banks

**GLOBAL FACT:** Commercial banks accept deposits, make loans, operate payment systems and manage liquidity. They earn income partly from the difference between interest received on loans and interest paid on deposits, as well as fees.

**PNG-SPECIFIC FACT:** BPNG regulates and licenses deposit-taking institutions under laws including the Central Banking Act 2000, Banks and Financial Institutions Act 2000 and Savings and Loan Societies Act 2015.[^1_17][^1_3]

### Central banking in PNG

BPNG’s primary monetary-policy objective under the Central Banking Act 2000 is **price stability**. Secondary objectives are financial stability, sustainable economic growth and development of PNG’s financial system.[^1_2][^1_1]

BPNG’s Monetary Policy Committee formulates and implements monetary policy, and the Bank issues semi-annual Monetary Policy Statements.[^1_1][^1_2]

### Current monetary-policy settings

**CURRENT FACT, dated March 2026:** BPNG Governor Elizabeth Genia stated that the Monetary Policy Committee maintained the **Kina Facility Rate at 5.0%** and the **Cash Reserve Requirement at 9.0%**. The exchange rate was described as the nominal anchor for inflation.[^1_18][^1_19]

**CURRENT FACT, IMF June 2026:** The IMF reported that PNG’s growth was projected to moderate to **3.8% in 2026** after an estimated **5.6% in 2025**, while headline inflation was expected to rise modestly to **4.8% in 2026**.[^1_8]

**Caution:** BPNG’s March 2026 outlook projected around 4.0% headline inflation, while the IMF’s June 2026 projection was 4.8%; both are forecasts, not final outcomes, and use different publication dates.[^1_8][^1_18]

### Lender of last resort and financial stability

The IMF’s 2025–2026 programme documentation refers to development of an emergency liquidity assistance framework under section 76 of the Central Banking Act, intended to operationalise a lender-of-last-resort regime introduced through the amended Central Banking Act in 2024. This is an important, current PNG-specific example of financial-stability policy.[^1_20][^1_7]

### Deposit protection

The retrieved sources do **not** establish a current, general PNG deposit-insurance scheme. Do not assume one exists. This is a priority research gap requiring verification from BPNG legislation and official publications.

## F. Foreign Exchange

### PNG kina and exchange-rate regime

**PNG-SPECIFIC FACT / CURRENT FACT:** BPNG describes maintaining relative stability in the exchange rate, interest rates and inflation as part of monetary policy. In March 2026, BPNG described the exchange rate as the nominal anchor and said it was continuing a gradual pace of exchange-rate adjustment.[^1_18][^1_2][^1_1]

The IMF describes PNG’s arrangement as a **crawl-like exchange-rate arrangement** and states that access to foreign exchange has improved due to central-bank reforms, reduced kina overvaluation and higher commodity prices.[^1_8]

### Why FX matters in PNG

PNG exports LNG, gold, copper, crude oil, coffee, cocoa and palm oil. These exports generate foreign-currency earnings, while imports, debt service and some business payments create foreign-currency demand.[^1_21][^1_6]

The IMF reported that PNG’s 2026 balance of payments was projected to be in deficit because higher financial-account outflows, mainly debt servicing, were expected to exceed a large current-account surplus.[^1_19][^1_18]

### Effects on households and businesses

- **Kina depreciation** can raise the kina cost of imported fuel, food, medicine and equipment.
- **Kina appreciation** can lower import costs but may reduce kina returns from export earnings.
- Exporters, importers, borrowers with foreign-currency debt and households buying imported goods all face currency risk.
- These are general economic mechanisms; PNG-specific magnitude estimates require current BPNG or IMF data.

## G. Commodities

### PNG’s export base

**PNG-SPECIFIC FACT / DATA DATE 2024:** The WTO’s 2026 trade policy review states that energy and mineral exports accounted for over **77% of PNG’s exports in 2024**, and that PNG’s export-commodity dependence index was high at **97%**.[^1_6]

Major export categories include LNG, crude petroleum, gold, copper, palm oil, coffee, cocoa, logs and other agricultural products.[^1_21][^1_6]

### Recent evidence

- BPNG reported total merchandise exports of **K11,334.0 million in the March quarter of 2024**; mineral exports including LNG and condensate were **K9,958.5 million**, or **87.9%** of merchandise exports in that quarter.[^1_22]
- The World Bank reported that PNG’s current-account surplus rose sharply in 2024 to **15.1% of GDP**, driven by strong mineral exports following the reopening of the Porgera gold mine and high copper and gold prices.[^1_21]
- The World Bank also noted that agriculture remains vital, with coffee, cocoa and palm oil supporting rural incomes and foreign exchange.[^1_21]


### Educational use

PNG is a strong case study for showing how global commodity prices affect:

- export revenue;
- government revenue;
- foreign-exchange availability;
- kina exchange-rate pressure;
- rural employment and incomes;
- government borrowing needs.

**Do not overstate causation:** commodity prices influence these outcomes, but taxation arrangements, production volumes, project ownership, weather, security, infrastructure and global demand also matter.

## H. Investment Concepts

### Core principles

| Concept | Research-based explanation |
| :-- | :-- |
| Saving vs investing | Saving preserves money for near-term needs; investing accepts uncertainty in pursuit of potential long-term growth. |
| Return | The gain or loss on an investment, which may come from interest, dividends, rent, price change or a combination. |
| Risk | The possibility that actual outcomes differ from expected outcomes, including loss of capital. |
| Diversification | Spreading money across different assets, sectors or countries to reduce exposure to any single outcome. |
| Liquidity | How quickly an asset can be converted to cash at a reasonable price. |
| Time horizon | The period before money is needed; longer horizons can allow more time to recover from temporary losses, but do not guarantee recovery. |
| Compounding | Returns earned on prior returns; powerful over long periods but also applies to debt. |
| Inflation | A general rise in prices that reduces purchasing power if returns do not keep pace. |
| Real vs nominal return | Nominal return is the stated return; real return adjusts for inflation. |
| Risk tolerance | A person’s willingness and financial capacity to accept variability or loss. |

**Editorial rule:** The book must not imply that investing guarantees profit, that diversification removes all risk, or that past performance predicts future returns.

### PNG relevance

PNG readers can understand investment concepts through local instruments: bank deposits, savings and loan society memberships, Treasury bills, listed PNGX shares, superannuation and mobile-money balances. Treasury bills are publicly accessible through BPNG auctions, including for investors outside Port Moresby.[^1_9]

## I. Risk

### Risk taxonomy

| Risk | Meaning | Simple example |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Market risk | Prices or values move against an investor | Share prices fall after poor company results |
| Credit risk | A borrower fails to pay interest or principal | A company defaults on a bond |
| Liquidity risk | An asset cannot be sold quickly at fair value | A thinly traded share cannot be sold without a large price discount |
| Interest-rate risk | Changing interest rates affect asset prices or borrowing costs | Existing bond prices generally fall when market interest rates rise |
| Inflation risk | Inflation reduces purchasing power | A fixed deposit may lose real value if inflation is higher |
| Currency risk | Exchange-rate movements change value | A kina-based investor holding US-dollar assets faces kina/USD movements |
| Operational risk | Internal failures, systems errors or process failures cause loss | A payment outage prevents transactions |
| Counterparty risk | The other side of a contract fails to perform | A derivatives counterparty cannot settle |
| Concentration risk | Too much exposure to one asset, sector or country | A portfolio made up only of one mining company |
| Systemic risk | Problems in one institution or market spread through the system | A bank failure triggers wider loss of confidence |
| Fraud risk | Deliberate deception causes loss | A fake investment platform collects deposits |
| Cyber risk | Digital attacks compromise systems or data | Phishing leads to unauthorised access to an account |

SCPNG’s investor-education material explicitly links capital-market regulation to protection against scams, deception, fraud and market manipulation.[^1_12][^1_14]

## J. Regulation

### Why regulate?

Financial regulation seeks to protect investors and consumers, maintain fair and orderly markets, require disclosure, reduce systemic risk, prevent fraud and manipulation, and support anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorism-financing systems. SCPNG describes its mandate as protecting investors, maintaining market integrity and preventing fraudulent or unfair practices.[^1_10][^1_14]

### PNG regulators

| Institution | Role |
| :-- | :-- |
| Bank of Papua New Guinea | Central bank; monetary policy, currency, banking supervision, payment systems, financial stability. [^1_1][^1_2][^1_17] |
| Securities Commission of PNG | Capital-market regulator; licenses participants, approves rules, monitors conduct, protects investors. [^1_5][^1_14] |
| Department of Treasury | Manages government debt and issues Treasury bills and bonds through BPNG as issuing agent. [^1_23][^1_11] |
| PNGX Markets | Operates the stock exchange and enforces listing and trading rules, subject to SCPNG approval. [^1_10][^1_24][^1_5] |

### International bodies

- **IMF:** supports macroeconomic and financial-sector stability; in PNG it supports reforms on debt sustainability, FX shortages, governance and climate resilience.[^1_8]
- **World Bank:** provides development finance, analysis and economic updates; its PNG work covers resilience, infrastructure, education, health and economic development.[^1_25]
- **BIS:** hosts central-bank cooperation and publishes central-bank speeches and research; it published BPNG Governor Genia’s March 2026 policy remarks.[^1_18]
- **WTO:** monitors trade policy; its 2026 PNG review provides export-concentration and trade-structure evidence.[^1_6]

## K. Financial Technology

### Established uses

- **Mobile money and digital payments:** relevant in PNG through licensed payment service providers; BPNG lists Digicel PNG Financial Services as a payment service provider.[^1_3]
- **Online banking and electronic payments:** increasingly important for bank customers, businesses and government payments.
- **Online trading:** can improve access but requires investor education, cybersecurity and verification of licensed intermediaries.
- **AI in finance:** can support fraud detection, credit assessment and customer service, but may introduce bias, model risk and data-protection concerns.


### Emerging or speculative uses

- **Blockchain and tokenisation:** may improve record-keeping and settlement in some settings, but widespread benefits are not guaranteed.
- **Cryptocurrencies and stablecoins:** high volatility, consumer-protection, fraud, cybersecurity and regulatory concerns require careful treatment.
- **Algorithmic and high-frequency trading:** mainly relevant to large, liquid markets; do not imply these are common or accessible in PNG.


### PNG crypto position

The retrieved official sources did **not** establish a complete, current PNG legal position on cryptocurrency ownership, trading, taxation or licensing. Do not state that crypto is legal, illegal, regulated or unregulated without direct verification from BPNG, IRC, SCPNG or PNG legislation. This is a high-priority research gap.

## N. International Financial System

### Key institutions

- **IMF:** crisis lending, surveillance, policy advice and balance-of-payments support. In PNG, IMF-supported EFF/ECF and RSF arrangements are active; the IMF completed the sixth EFF/ECF reviews and third RSF review in June 2026.[^1_8]
- **World Bank:** long-term development finance, knowledge and technical assistance.[^1_25]
- **BIS:** central-bank cooperation, banking standards and research.[^1_18]
- **WTO:** trade rules and trade-policy review.[^1_6]
- **Major central banks:** influence global interest rates, dollar liquidity and risk appetite.
- **International banks and investment funds:** move capital across borders through loans, bonds, equities and portfolio investment.
- **Credit-rating agencies:** assess default risk; their views can affect borrowing costs, but ratings are opinions and have limitations.


### How money moves internationally

Money moves through trade payments, foreign direct investment, portfolio investment, bank lending, remittances, sovereign borrowing and official assistance. For PNG, export receipts, import payments, debt service, mining and petroleum-company flows, aid and remittances are especially relevant.[^1_7][^1_18][^1_6]

Research v1 is broadly sound on PNG institutional structure, BPNG’s mandate, Treasury securities, PNGX’s role, commodity dependence and the need to date all statistics. Its strongest areas are the use of BPNG, SCPNG, PNGX, Treasury, IMF, World Bank and WTO sources.[^2_1][^2_2][^2_3][^2_4][^2_5]

However, the audit identifies **four material corrections**:

1. **Bank numbers must be categorised.** “Four commercial banks” and “seven licensed banks” are not necessarily contradictory, but Research v1 did not distinguish clearly between commercial banks and the wider set of licensed financial institutions. BPNG’s official list as at 29 July 2025 identifies seven commercial banks, alongside 13 licensed financial institutions, 18 savings and loan societies, payment providers and other regulated entities.[^2_4]

The eBook must not present total market capitalisation as the value of PNG’s domestic corporate sector.[^2_7][^2_8]
4. **Crypto claims must be narrowed.** BPNG’s official position is that it has **not licensed Virtual Asset Service Providers or cryptocurrency-based investment platforms** and that such schemes are not authorised or regulated by BPNG. This is not the same as proving that private ownership or use of cryptocurrency is illegal. The book should avoid both “crypto is legal and regulated” and “crypto is banned.”[^2_9]

Overall recommendation: **carry Research v1 forward with the corrections below**, replace outdated or weakly sourced figures, and add explicit “current as at” dates throughout.

## 3. 2026 Updates

### BPNG and monetary policy

**CURRENT — September 2026:** BPNG’s Monetary Policy Committee maintained the **Kina Facility Rate at 5.0%** and the **Cash Reserve Requirement at 9.0%** at its September 2026 meeting.[^2_10][^2_11]

**CURRENT — framework:** BPNG issues two Monetary Policy Statements each year, on 31 March and 30 September. Since 2024 amendments to the Central Banking Act, an independent Monetary Policy Committee formulates policy and announces decisions approximately every six weeks. BPNG uses the KFR as a policy signal, conducts weekly open-market operations, and uses the Cash Reserve Requirement to manage liquidity.[^2_12]

**CURRENT — exchange rate:** BPNG continues a **crawl-like exchange-rate arrangement** and weekly FX auctions, with auction volumes determined by the MPC according to liquidity and outstanding FX orders.[^2_13][^2_14]

**CURRENT — inflation:** BPNG’s September 2026 outlook maintained a headline-inflation forecast of around **4% for 2026**, with underlying measures expected to remain at or just below that level through 2027–2028.[^2_11][^2_15]

**Interpretation:** BPNG states that the exchange rate has recently played a stronger role than the KFR as the nominal anchor for inflation, and that restoring greater FX flexibility and convertibility is part of its reform agenda. This is an official policy interpretation, not a prediction that the kina will depreciate or appreciate.[^2_15]

### PNGX and capital markets

**CURRENT — structure:** PNGX Markets remains PNG’s national stock exchange and operates listing, trading, clearing and settlement services. Its market operates on business days from 9:00 am to 4:00 pm through its PNGX Market Trading System.[^2_16]

**CURRENT — listings:** PNGX’s official listing page currently shows **13 companies**. SCPNG reported **12 listed companies as of January 2026**, comprising eight local domestic entities and four foreign dual-listed entities. The difference is consistent with a later listing; the book should date whichever count it uses.[^2_6][^2_7]

**NEEDS VERIFICATION — market capitalisation:** The K135–140 billion January 2026 estimate and the US\$3.6 billion June 2026 non-dual-listed estimate are useful context but should be replaced or confirmed with PNGX’s official market-statistics page before publication.[^2_8][^2_17][^2_7]

**Interpretation:** PNG’s equity market is small and concentrated. A few large companies—including BSP and Santos—account for much of total market value, and several listings are dual-listed foreign companies. This affects how “market capitalisation” should be explained to readers.[^2_7][^2_8]

### Government securities

**CURRENT — instruments:** BPNG issues Treasury bills on behalf of the Government to finance budget deficits. Treasury bills are short-term discounted debt instruments; current issued tenors are 182-day and 364-day bills, auctioned weekly.[^2_2]

**CURRENT — bonds:** Treasury manages longer-term domestic borrowing through Treasury bonds/inscribed stock, with auction notices and historical tender results published by the Department of Treasury.[^2_18][^2_19]

**NEEDS VERIFICATION:** Current minimum investment amounts, tap facilities, latest auction yields, domestic-versus-external debt split and debt-to-GDP should be taken from the latest Treasury auction results, BPNG auction notices and IMF Article IV tables—not carried forward from Research v1.[^2_19][^2_20][^2_18]

### Financial inclusion and digital finance

**CURRENT — strategy:** PNG’s National Financial Inclusion Strategy is now in its third iteration, covering **2023–2027**. BPNG/CEFI state that the strategy aims to enable more than two million new bank accounts, expand access points, and scale mobile and digital banking, with emphasis on women and rural communities.[^2_21]

**CURRENT — literacy:** BPNG/CEFI report that more than **250,000 Papua New Guineans** have received financial-literacy training, including approximately **120,000 women**.[^2_15][^2_21]

**CURRENT — inclusion target:** The NFIS aims to include at least **50% of Papua New Guineans in the formal banking network by 2030**.[^2_21]

**CAUTION:** “Bank account,” “formal financial-service access,” “registered mobile-money account” and “active user” are different measures. The eBook must not merge them.

### Consumer protection and scams

**CURRENT — official warning:** BPNG issued a public advisory on 7 July 2025 warning about unregulated “investment opportunities” on WhatsApp, Facebook and Telegram, including schemes promising extreme short-term returns. BPNG stated that these schemes are not licensed, authorised or regulated by the Bank, and that BPNG has not licensed VASPs or cryptocurrency-based investment platforms.[^2_9]

**CURRENT — reporting:** BPNG advises the public to verify a provider’s licence or registration with BPNG and to report suspicious activity to `info@bankpng.gov.pg` or the nearest law-enforcement agency.[^2_9]

## 6. PNG Institutional Map

### Bank of Papua New Guinea

**Role:** PNG’s central bank. It issues currency, acts as banker to government, formulates monetary policy, supervises banks and licensed financial institutions, oversees payment systems, manages FX-market operations and supports financial stability.[^2_22][^2_25][^2_1][^2_12]

**Current policy facts:** KFR 5.0%; CRR 9.0%; crawl-like FX arrangement; weekly FX auctions; two annual Monetary Policy Statements; MPC decisions approximately every six weeks.[^2_10][^2_12][^2_13]

### Commercial banks

**Role:** Accept deposits, provide loans, payments, foreign-exchange services and digital banking. BPNG listed seven commercial banks as at 29 July 2025.[^2_4]

**Editorial distinction:** “Seven banks” is a licensing count. It does not by itself prove that banking assets, deposits or loans are evenly distributed among them.

### Other licensed financial institutions

**Role:** BPNG lists 13 licensed financial institutions, including microfinance-oriented providers. They broaden access to basic financial services but may have different licences, products and geographic reach from commercial banks.[^2_4]

### Savings and loan societies

**Role:** BPNG lists 18 savings and loan societies authorised to accept member deposits and lend to members. They are useful PNG examples of community-based financial intermediation.[^2_4]

### Securities Commission of PNG

**Role:** Independent conduct regulator for PNG capital markets, established under the Securities Commission Act 2015. It licenses capital-market participants, approves market rules, monitors conduct and promotes investor protection.[^2_3][^2_26]

### PNGX Markets

**Role:** Operates PNG’s only stock exchange; provides listing, trading, clearing and settlement services. Investors access listed securities through authorised brokers rather than directly through the exchange.[^2_3][^2_16]

### Department of Treasury

**Role:** Manages government debt, issues Treasury bills and bonds through BPNG as issuing agent, and publishes auction results and debt-financing plans.[^2_20][^2_18][^2_19]

### National Statistical Office

**Role:** Primary source for population, household, labour and national-accounts statistics. Research v1 did not sufficiently use NSO data for population, GDP or household financial-access measures; this should be corrected in the Research Master.

### Superannuation and insurance

**Role:** BPNG’s licensing framework includes life-insurance businesses and licensed superannuation trustees. Superannuation pools long-term retirement savings and can be a major institutional investor, but current asset, membership and investment-allocation data must be sourced before use.[^2_4]

## 12. Recommended Book Implications

- **Use PNG institutions as the entry point.** Start with familiar institutions—banks, mobile money, savings and loan societies, superannuation, PNGX and government securities—before introducing global market structures.
- **Explain categories carefully.** Readers should learn that “bank,” “licensed financial institution,” “savings and loan society” and “payment provider” are different regulated categories.[^2_4]
- **Treat PNGX as a small, concentrated market.** Explain dual listings, market concentration and why total market capitalisation can overstate the size of locally controlled listed businesses.[^2_7][^2_8]
- **Make commodity dependence central.** PNG’s export structure means global prices for LNG, gold, copper, oil and agricultural commodities affect export earnings, foreign exchange, government revenue and economic confidence.[^2_24][^2_5]
- **Explain exchange rates through daily life.** Use imports, exports, fuel, food, business costs and exporter earnings—not abstract currency charts alone. BPNG’s crawl-like arrangement and FX auctions provide accurate PNG context.[^2_14][^2_13]
- **Teach scam recognition, not fear.** BPNG’s warning about unrealistic returns, social-media investment schemes and unlicensed providers is a strong, PNG-specific basis for consumer-protection content.[^2_9]
- **Avoid investment advice.** Present risk, diversification, liquidity and time horizon as concepts; do not recommend shares, crypto, deposits or any other asset.
- **Label every number.** A future writer should be able to answer: *What do we know, how do we know it, how current is it, and how confidently can we put it in the book?*
